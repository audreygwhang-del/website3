Question:
“Can I use indirect measurements to derive the mass and radius of the Earth from multiple freefall measurements and the sunset?”

Why it matters:
Being able to use indirect measurements to derive the mass of an object is important since it can help us measure masses that seem almost impossible to measure–like the Earth’s mass. By proving that this method works, we can use it to measure the mass of other very large objects. For example, scientists could find the mass of Mars by using measurements of its gravitational acceleration and radius without having to directly place the planet on a scale, which is impossible.

What are the underlying theories/ laws?

State a hypothesis

Materials

  • Sunglasses
  • Phone (for stopwatch and slo-mo videos)
  • Notebook + pencil for recording
  • Eraser (for dropping)
  • Measure tape

Sunset Measurement:

  1. Lie down facing the west.
  2. Watch for the sun to dip just below the horizon (make sure to wear sunglasses).
  3. Start the stopwatch once it has just gone past the horizon line.
  4. Quickly stand up.
  5. Stop the stopwatch once the sun dips below the horizon again.

Freefall Measurement

  1. Find an eraser to drop, and choose a place to drop it that is at least 5m tall.
  2. Measure the height from which the object was dropped (in meters) using your measuring tape.
  3. Station a “dropper” at the top, and a “timer” at the bottom with a phone, notebook, and a writing utensil to record the times.
  4. The “dropper” should signal to the “timer” and then drop the eraser.
  5. Simultaneously, the “timer” should start the stopwatch as the “dropper” releases the eraser.
  6. The “timer” should then stop the stopwatch once the eraser hits the ground.
  7. Record the drop time.
  8. Repeat the drop procedure 14 more times.

– Slo-mo Method (Used for final 5 measurements) –

  1. The “timer” should start a slo-mo video just before the “dropper” releases the eraser
  2. The “timer” should stop the video just after the eraser hits the ground, ensuring that the object remains in frame throughout.
  3. Record the drop time.
  4. Repeat this procedure 4 more times, recording the measurements
  1. Find the average of all drop times to use in later measurements.

What went wrong:

My group and I switched methods partway through, using a stopwatch for the first 15 drops and then switching to slo-mo for the last 5, since slo-mo helped remove the possibility of a reaction time error, and was therefore more reliable. Using the stopwatch made our combined average (stopwatch + slo-mo time points) 0.862 seconds less accurate than just the slo-mo average 1.05 seconds; Using these data points to find the gravitational acceleration gave us 13.5 m/s^2 (all time points used)  and 9.1m/s^2 (only slo-mo timepoints used), with the latter being much closer to the actual gravitational acceleration of the Earth:
  2. I also believe that I also made an error when I was timing watching the sunset to capture the amount of time it took for it to fully set while standing up after crouching down. I believe this since I had a couple of trees and houses blocking my view of a perfect sunset, which could’ve thrown off my timing. My group and I made an error when measuring the height from where we dropped the eraser to where the eraser hit the ground, so we had to use another group’s data that dropped their item from the same stairwell. This could be a contention since the other group could’ve dropped their item from a different height point, and they could’ve made some errors when measuring the height.
